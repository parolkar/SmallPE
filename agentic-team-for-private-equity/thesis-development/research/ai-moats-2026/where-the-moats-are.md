## Thesis

The AI capability race split into two separate races, and conflating them is
the most common diligence error we see today. **Open-weight models won
reach.** Led by Chinese labs -- Qwen, DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM, Ling -- after
Meta's 2026 retreat to closed development, open models now carry the large
majority of commodity inference volume at a fraction of closed cost.
**Closed frontier labs won revenue.** OpenAI, Anthropic and Google tripled
enterprise spend capture in a year by monetizing a narrow, persistent
capability lead on the hard work. We think the second race determines where
investable value accrues -- and the asset worth underwriting is no longer
the model at all.

## Why the moat moved off the model

The gap between open and closed frontier capability has held at roughly
**four months** for a year running (three months in late 2025) -- a stable,
non-closing lead. Model weights are now a **perishable commodity**, not a
durable asset: a four-month lead is worth nothing on a routine task and a
large multiple on one where the model is the bottleneck for an expert's day
-- long-horizon agentic work, professional judgment, long-context retrieval.
That is precisely where enterprises pay, and it explains the chart above:
open models dominate usage, closed models dominate the P&L.

What frontier labs now race to acquire is not better weights but three
inputs no release commoditizes: **proprietary domain data** (the operating
history of a real business); **rigorous, task-specific evaluation** (what
"good enough" means for a given job, not a leaderboard score); and **graded
agentic trajectories** (verified, step-by-step records of real work, the
training signal for the next generation of agents). The clearest proof of
scarcity: a market worth an estimated $6B+ a year has emerged of vendors
selling frontier labs synthetic, sandboxed replicas of enterprise software --
fake Jira boards, fake EMRs, fake core-banking systems -- purely to
manufacture trajectories for post-training. Labs are paying real money to
approximate what operating companies already possess natively. That is the
moat, and it sits on operating companies' own books, not in a lab's weights.

Ecosystem leadership migrated geopolitically alongside this shift. Meta, once
the undisputed open-weight leader, shipped a closed flagship model in 2026
and ceded that ground; Chinese labs now account for a plurality of
open-model downloads, up sharply from a year earlier. Google, by contrast,
leaned into permissive open licensing for its small/edge models -- ceding
the commodity tier on purpose to cement its position at the frontier.

## What this changes in how we underwrite

| Dimension | Thin AI wrapper | Data / evaluation moat |
|---|---|---|
| Owns | Rented intelligence + UI on a frontier API | Proprietary data, eval harness, graded trajectories |
| Defensibility | Erodes in ~4 months as rivals route elsewhere | Compounds -- not reproducible by a competitor |
| Margin path | Exposed to vendor pricing swings | Improves via eval-driven routing to cheaper models |
| Our stance | Discount terminal value | Premium multiple for a durable, ownable asset |

We no longer credit a target for "using AI," nor for which model it wraps --
that layer commoditizes on a predictable clock. Three changes follow: we
**discount terminal value** on wrapper-only revenue; we underwrite
**model-routing savings** (~5x cost reduction on commodity tasks routed to
open models) as realizable EBITDA upside, not blue-sky; and **no deal reaches
IC** without an evidenced answer to *what does this company own that a
frontier-lab release can't erase overnight?*

## Diligence: six tests for any AI-exposed target

| Test | What we require |
|---|---|
| **1. Evaluation harness** | A real eval suite run against the company's own workload, cost-per-task attached -- not a vendor benchmark in a deck |
| **2. Data provenance** | Owned or exclusively licensed, not merely scraped or touched |
| **3. Rented vs. defensible** | Share of workflows on a single API; portability to a second model family without a rewrite |
| **4. Trajectory capture** | Workflow logs retained, structured, and graded against verified outcomes -- not just stored |
| **5. Lab-licensing claims** | Carried at zero until a signed, disclosed contract exists -- no proven resale market yet |
| **6. Log governance** | Trajectory/prompt logs diligenced like IP: ownership, retention, DPAs, sub-processor terms |

This is now a standing workstream in our diligence process on every deal with
material AI exposure, with named owners and evidence requirements -- not a
bolt-on to IT diligence.

## Where we hunt, and what we avoid

Commoditized model capability sharpens our existing lower-middle-market
conviction. We favor **incumbent, "boring" operators** -- specialty
manufacturers, services firms, healthcare operators, logistics companies --
sitting on years of proprietary operating data over AI-native startups
pitched on branding; this is exactly the data frontier labs spend billions
trying to synthetically approximate because they cannot buy or scrape the
real thing. **Red flags:** single-vendor model dependency with no
proprietary data; no internal evaluation capability; a commoditizable
service wrapped in a chatbot; a thesis pitched on branding over dataset
depth.

## A second front: the compute layer

A parallel dislocation is forming one layer down, in the infrastructure that
serves the models. Investor commentary we are tracking -- an October 2026 X
exchange between Chamath Palihapitiya and Elon Musk -- describes hyperscalers
as slow, bureaucratic compute buyers (months-long NDAs, multi-year escrow)
losing "energized land" deals to faster operators, and warns that 2027
compute coming online faster than it can be absorbed will push hyperscalers
to prioritize their own workloads and ration third parties through
proprietary routers that take a margin -- citing a rumored instance in Ant's
IPO filing of a hyperscaler telling Ant it had no spare capacity. Musk adds a
financing angle: debt markets have reportedly closed to AI-infrastructure
borrowers without revenue independent of AI data centers -- citing SpaceX's
$40B GPU-related debt, backed by Starlink and launch revenue rather than the
GPUs -- and predicts consolidation among the 200+ neocloud operators that
lack it.

*(Source: Palihapitiya [@chamath] & Musk [@elonmusk],
[X thread, Oct 2026](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2114434646914068862).)*
We treat this as directionally consistent with the thesis above --
commoditized capacity rewards whoever holds a durable asset, data at the
application layer, power/land/balance-sheet quality at the infrastructure
layer -- but flag it as one unverified social-media exchange between two
investors with direct financial exposure (Chamath's own land portfolio
benefits if this plays out; Musk's company is the credit example cited). We
underwrite the pattern, not the 2027 date or the unnamed exception. **What
this means for us:** for any target with AI-infrastructure exposure, we now
diligence counterparty concentration and whether its AI-infrastructure debt
is backed by revenue independent of the buildout itself.

## Governance: the moat is also a liability filter

Open-weight proliferation brought a wave of derivative models with safety
guardrails stripped out, circulating freely at scale. Enterprise capital has
priced this in -- governance maturity is now a buying criterion behind spend
concentrating in better-governed closed labs. We treat this as a diligence
lens: we document model provenance and misuse exposure for every AI-enabled
portfolio company; require consent, retention limits, IP treatment, and a
named owner before any trajectory data is monetized; and underwrite without
assuming the outcome of the live open-weight policy fight. We hold our own
research to the same bar: no risk-mitigation credit for unverified practices.

## Scorecard: what we believe, one year out

| Claim | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Open weights would lap closed models | Did not happen | Gap stable at ~4 months for a year |
| Nobody pays for closed models | Did not happen | Spend tripled to ~$37B; ~90% in three closed labs |

| The model itself is the moat | False | Moat relocated to data, evaluation, trajectories |

## Supporting Analysis & FAQ

**Q1: How reliable is the "~4 month" gap, and could it close tomorrow?** It
reflects a capability index tracked consistently for a year, widening
slightly (three to four months) rather than narrowing -- a steady-state
window, not a trend to zero. Limitation: an index aggregates many benchmarks
and can mask task-specific variance. Our response is not to bet on the
index, but to require every target to run its own evaluation (Test #1).

**Q2: Is the "data/evaluation moat" framing just a way to call AI wrappers
overvalued?** No -- it is the mechanism. A wrapper's advantage is
observably time-bound: once a cheaper model clears the bar, its edge
evaporates within roughly the capability-gap window. Only the portion of
value attributable to "which model we call" gets discounted toward zero --
not owned data or graded operational exhaust.

**Q3: Is trajectory data actually monetizable, or speculative?** Demand
evidence is solid -- a multi-billion-dollar vendor market sells labs
*synthetic* workflow approximations, which only makes sense if the real
thing has real training value. We have no evidence of companies licensing
*real* trajectory data under disclosed contracts, and flag that gap
explicitly (Test #5). Our stance: capture internally; treat external
licensing as unproven upside, not underwritten value.

**Q4: Should we avoid thin-wrapper companies entirely?** No -- we discount,
we don't exclude by category. A wrapper can migrate into the defensible
category by building an eval harness and accumulating graded outcome data
over time. We underwrite the moat that exists plus a credible path to build
one.

**Q5: What changes operationally, beyond the memo?** Three things, already
in effect: AI Capability & Data Assets is a standing diligence workstream
with named owners on every AI-exposed deal; models carry a terminal-value
discount on wrapper revenue and a margin line for routing savings; and
sourcing now favors data-rich incumbents, redirecting Helios's effort.
